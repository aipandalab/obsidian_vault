**Nathan Lambert:** The questions had many layers. I’d start with a relatively easy one to answer: "Why are you releasing these models?" "Why build your own models?" Part of it is corporate culture, but I think mostly it’s because the environment they are in is much more confined than what is taught in schools; it’s more about "I built the thing." Also, China doesn’t have as strong a podcast ecosystem as the US, where researchers can gain recognition and even influence company direction through increased exposure. The overall vibe is much more pragmatic. Sometimes when writing, I mix this pragmatism with "humility," but actually, someone can be humble, while at the same time, they might just be in an environment where they aren't used to being asked certain questions.

**Chen Qian:** You wrote that Chinese companies have a "technology ownership mentality." Can you explain that?

**Nathan Lambert:** This ties back to the super app idea. They just feel they can build the thing, and build it better. If successful, they keep investing money, keep improving efficiency, and tune it for their specific scenarios. This is very similar to the path many Chinese domestic industries took to become some of the largest global industries: do it yourself if you can, rather than buying someone else's service. That's how you control the long-term trajectory. It's like—I think it was Wang Dan in _Breakneck_ who said that Chinese capitalism is more intense than America's, because they think over longer cycles about what will bring the best profit margins, the strongest product capabilities, and competitive advantages. If you rely on someone else's service, it's hard to truly beat them. In a highly competitive environment, you want to do everything best, and ideally, do it yourself. So I understand this as a "keep it in our own hands" mentality. For these companies, building a model isn't yet an entirely unaffordable expense. I estimate they might spend $500 million to a few billion dollars a year on these models. It’s a lot of money, of course, but it hasn't reached the point where these labs can't afford it. If model capabilities continue to scale up, the market will likely eventually consolidate, because capability improvements look somewhat logarithmic to investment—you invest more, the model gets stronger, but with diminishing marginal returns. Right now is a very interesting phase. I remember interviewing Richard from Ant Bailing; he said it only took them six months to build the team making large models. Domestic labs already have Huawei chips and are designing inference solutions for them. In a sense, this has already happened. The remaining questions are how big the scale is, and to what extent people deem it acceptable. So the bottleneck is really Huawei's production capacity.

**Chen Qian:** Right. And the ecosystem. Whether models can easily run on these chips—that is, what is the corresponding CUDA ecosystem like.

**Nathan Lambert:** I am very bullish on AI demand continuing to grow, so demand will create this necessity.

**Chen Qian:** You mentioned that China's weakness in the AI data industry is more important than people realize. Can you elaborate on that?

**Nathan Lambert:** One of the things that surprised me most was that China's data industry is nowhere near as developed as America's. In the current environment in the US, companies like Anthropic and OpenAI will say they have billions of dollars in data budgets to buy training environments from external vendors. We asked Chinese companies, and they said, "No, we basically don't have that kind of thing." Some do it internally. People mention ByteDance or Alibaba, companies large enough to build internal data teams. But almost no one says, "We go to external vendors to buy data." Whereas in the US, this is incredibly common. It's practically the standard playbook for frontier labs. Realistically, training a model sometimes means: whichever part of the model is weak, you go buy data for that part to see if you can pull the performance up. Of course, they also do more exciting research, but this boring data engineering is extremely important. And this dynamic doesn't seem to exist in China. This is the biggest difference I saw. OpenAI researchers obviously have more compute than Chinese researchers, so Chinese startups might work on different problems and look more efficient. But data doesn't inherently _have_ to be different, yet it _is_ very different.

Right now, the understanding of US frontier models is that they will become knowledge work tools. For a typical knowledge worker like me, I use Claude Code and Codex to handle most of my work now. People will see them enter medical, legal, consulting, financial fields, etc. And a lot of the training data for these models in these fields is acquired through networks of data companies. They pay professionals to create training data. The US has a complete market: Scale AI, Mercor, Surge, Turing, and many other companies. The market is pushing them to shift from doing offshore preference data for RLHF to highly skilled labor industries. The scale is massive. The amounts model labs pay for this are in the billions of dollars. They do this because it's genuinely valuable; it makes models stronger at these agentic tasks. So the question is, can Chinese labs open up the domestic market for their own agents? If people truly use their models for these things, the demand will give them the ability to buy this expensive data. Conversely, if Chinese AI researchers and knowledge workers all say, "I just use Claude," then Claude has even more reason to keep doing these things in the US, and the data industry will remain in the US. I talk to friends doing post-training in labs, and they say these data companies are truly crucial for getting incremental performance gains across various domains. If China doesn't have such an industry, they must be very creative. You might have AI researchers construct a financial task environment themselves, like building an Excel spreadsheet. But that's not a task AI researchers are naturally good at. That's another world.
